Purpose of this document
------------------------
To state, plainly and with sources, which parts of the 3D MBE render are
physics-faithful, which are idealized, and which are deliberately
exaggerated for visualization. This is a reference for anyone using the
simulation for teaching, review, or as a mental model of a real MBE reactor.

NOTE: Citations were verified against standard MBE textbooks (Cho, Herman &
Sitter, Parker, Panish & Temkin) and the primary literature (Cho & Arthur
1975, Foxon & Joyce 1977, Neave et al. 1983, Arthur 2002). Where a claim is
plausible-but-unsourced, that is stated explicitly. Use this document as a
checklist, not as a bibliography.

1. OVERVIEW — WHAT THE SIMULATION DEPICTS
================================================================================

  Method:         Molecular Beam Epitaxy (MBE) — solid-source, UHV
  Reactor:        Vertical cylindrical chamber, conical top, top-mounted
                  substrate manipulator with rotation
  Growth species: Ga, Al, As (K-cells, effusion)
  Substrate:      GaAs (100), 2" wafer
  Growth temp:    580 °C
  Growth rate:    ~1.0 ML/s for GaAs (≈ 1 µm/hr)
  As:Ga ratio:    ~15:1 BEP overpressure
  Product:        GaAs/Al0.3Ga0.7As double-heterostructure laser:
                    GaAs buffer                1.0 µm
                    Al0.3Ga0.7As lower clad    0.5 µm
                    GaAs active layer (DH)     0.1 µm
                    Al0.3Ga0.7As upper clad    0.5 µm
                    -------------------------------------
                    Total epilayer             2.1 µm

The DH structure is the same one grown by LPE, chosen so the two sims can be
compared. MBE produces it with atomic-level control and RHEED-monitored
layer-by-layer growth, which LPE does not.


================================================================================
2. GROWTH MECHANISM — WHY MBE IS DIFFERENT FROM LPE
================================================================================

PHYSICAL BASIS (well-established)
---------------------------------
In MBE, elemental sources are heated in effusion cells ("Knudsen cells") to
produce directed thermal beams. These beams impinge on a heated substrate in
UHV, where the arriving atoms adsorb, diffuse on the surface, and incorporate
into the crystal lattice.

  • Growth is fundamentally kinetic, not thermodynamic. The near-absence of
    gas-phase collisions means atoms arrive ballistically (mean free path
    >> chamber dimensions). Growth rate and composition are set by the
    ARRIVAL RATE (flux) of each species, not by equilibrium phase relations
    as in LPE.

  • Surface diffusion controls crystalline quality. At 580 °C, Ga and Al have
    diffusion lengths of order hundreds of nm on GaAs (100); As desorption is
    negligible under As overpressure. This is why MBE achieves atomically
    abrupt interfaces.

  • As overpressure (~10-20:1 As:Ga BEP) is essential. Without it, Ga
    accumulates as droplets on the surface; with it, Ga incorporation is
    limited by the Ga arrival rate and the surface stays stoichiometric.

WHY UHV
-------
  • The residual gas molecules must not exceed the arriving beam flux by
    more than a few percent. At 5×10⁻¹¹ Torr, the background impingement
    rate on the substrate is ~1 ML/hr, three orders of magnitude below a
    typical 1 ML/s growth flux.
  • "Ultra-high vacuum" is conventionally <10⁻⁹ Torr. MBE base pressures
    of 10⁻¹⁰ to 10⁻¹¹ Torr are standard on modern systems, achieved by
    ion + Ti sublimation + cryopumps and baking the chamber to ~150-200 °C.

3. VACUUM SYSTEM AND PUMPING
================================================================================

WHAT THE SIMULATION SHOWS
-------------------------
  • Base pressure:              5×10⁻¹¹ Torr (UHV)
  • Growth pressure:            ~2×10⁻¹⁰ Torr (rising during growth)
  • Load-lock pressure:         760 Torr → 5×10⁻⁷ Torr (before transfer)
  • Pump-down to base:          two-stage — roughing (760 → 0.1 Torr) then
                                turbo (0.1 → 5×10⁻⁶ Torr)
  • Visible pumps:
      Ion pump                  (0.42 R cylinder, side-mounted, cooling fins)
      Turbo pump                (0.30 R, bottom, before high-vac reached)
  • RGA:                        quadrupole residual gas analyzer (visible)
  • Ion gauge:                  Bayard-Alpert style (glows when P < 10⁻⁴)

WHY THIS IS CORRECT
-------------------
Modern MBE systems use three pumping stages:
  1. Cryopump or turbo — removes most gas during initial pump-down, down to
     ~10⁻⁷ Torr.
  2. Titanium sublimation pump — periodically flashed to getter residual
     gases (H2, CO, H2O) onto the chamber walls. Brings chamber to 10⁻¹⁰.
  3. Ion pump — continuously operating, maintains 10⁻¹⁰-10⁻¹¹ Torr.
Additionally, the chamber is lined with LN2-cooled cryopanels which actively
pump H2O and other condensables.

The load lock is a separate small chamber that allows wafer transfer without
venting the growth chamber. It is pumped by its own small turbo pump to
~10⁻⁷ Torr before the gate valve opens; during transfer, growth chamber
pressure rises temporarily (10× in the sim — real systems show 10-100×
short excursions).

The RGA is a quadrupole mass spectrometer that monitors background gas
species — particularly mass 18 (H2O), 28 (N2/CO), 44 (CO2). Used to detect
leaks and to verify bake completion. The ion gauge (Bayard-Alpert) reads
pressure continuously but is typically switched on only below 10⁻⁴ Torr
(the filament would burn out at higher pressure).

SOURCES
-------
Herman & Sitter (1996), Chapter 3 — "Vacuum Technology for MBE."

O'Hanlon, J.F. (2003). A User's Guide to Vacuum Technology, 3rd ed. Wiley.
    — Standard reference for ion pump, Ti-sub, and cryopump operation.

Panish, M.B. & Temkin, H. (1993). Gas Source Molecular Beam Epitaxy.
  Springer. Chapter 2.
    — Vacuum requirements and pump-down procedures, generally applicable
      to solid-source MBE as well.

Vendor data (illustrative, not verified):
  Veeco / Applied EPI / Riber MBE system specifications
  for GEN-930, GEN-2000, Riber 412, Riber 6000.

DELIBERATE SIMPLIFICATIONS
--------------------------
  • Pump-down is compressed to ~10 s of demo time. Real systems take
    4-24 hours for base pressure after chamber opening.
  • The individual contributions of cryo, Ti-sub, and ion pumps are lumped
    into one pressure curve.
  • The ion gauge and RGA are illustrative; their readouts are not
    independently simulated.
  • Bake-out (chamber walls at 150-200 °C for 24-72 h) is not depicted —
    its effect is already present in the "base pressure" the sim shows.
  • Venting with dry N2 is mentioned in the cycle but not fully animated.

4. CHAMBER GEOMETRY
================================================================================

WHAT THE SIMULATION SHOWS
-------------------------
  • Vertical cylindrical body (radius 1.5 model units, height 3.3)
  • Conical top section (radius 1.5 → 0.75, height 0.6)
  • Top CF flange (radius 0.85) for the substrate manipulator
  • Bottom CF flange (radius 1.65) for the source flange
  • 8 ports around the equator (blank feedthroughs)
  • Front viewport (radius 0.36, with glass)
  • Rear load lock (radius 0.38 × 0.65 long)
  • LN2 cryoshroud (inner radius 1.28, dark inner surface)

WHY THIS IS CORRECT
-------------------
MBE growth chambers are vertical cylinders (or "vertical + conical top")
because the substrate faces DOWN toward the source flange, which is at the
bottom. The geometry has multiple design constraints:
  • Source flange at bottom: all K-cells mount from below, pointing up at
    the substrate. This means the wafer's growth face is on its BOTTOM.
  • Manipulator from top: substrate holder and rotation shaft come down
    through the top flange.
  • Conical top: wide enough at the top to accept a large CF flange for the
    manipulator, narrow enough to maintain chamber integrity.
  • Cryopanel inner shroud: captures water and condensables that would
    otherwise outgas into the growth environment.
  • RHEED needs two ports at grazing angles to the substrate — typically
    one on the equator and one near the manipulator flange, or two on the
    equator at +30° and -30° to the substrate normal.
  • Load lock on the side or rear: transfers wafers without venting.

SOURCES
-------
Parker (1985), Chapter 2 — "Equipment."
Herman & Sitter (1996), Chapter 4 — "MBE System Components."
Cho & Arthur (1975), Section 3 — "Apparatus."

DELIBERATE SIMPLIFICATIONS
--------------------------
  • Chamber dimensions are compressed. A real 3" MBE has a chamber body
    ~60-90 cm tall × 40-60 cm diameter; the sim shows an exaggerated
    picture. Ratios are approximately correct.
  • Only 3 K-cells shown (Ga, Al, As). Real systems often have 6-10
    (adding dopants like Si, Be, C; plus additional group-III cells for
    grading, and sometimes a second As for cracker operation).
  • The cryopanel is shown as a solid inner shroud. Real cryopanels are
    often segmented plates with LN2 flow channels, and sometimes have a
    viewport cutout.
  • Conflat (CF) flange details (knife edges, copper gaskets, specific
    bolt patterns) are stylized. Real CF flanges have 6-24 bolts in a
    specific pattern, and the gasket is visible as a thin copper ring.

5. EFFUSION CELLS (K-CELLS)
================================================================================

WHAT THE SIMULATION SHOWS
-------------------------
Each cell has:
  • Water-cooled outer jacket (steel, radius 0.13, with copper cooling
    fittings visible)
  • Pyrolytic boron nitride (PBN) crucible (radius 0.085) inside
  • Tantalum heater coil (helix, 6 turns, radius 0.10) between jacket
    and crucible
  • Conical tip converging to ~0.045 R aperture
  • Pivoting shutter at the tip (hinged at +0.22 from axis)
  • Green LED indicator on shutter (lit when open)
  • W-5%Re/W-26%Re thermocouple stub visible

Operating temperatures:
  Ga cell:  980 °C  → ≈1 ML/s Ga flux
  Al cell: 1150 °C  → ≈0.4 ML/s Al flux (for Al0.3 composition)
  As cell:  320 °C  → As4 beam, ~15:1 As:Ga BEP

WHY THIS IS CORRECT
-------------------
K-cell design is standardized across the industry:
  • PBN crucibles: pyrolytic boron nitride is the standard because it is
    chemically inert to Ga, Al, In, and As at operating temperature, does
    not react with the charge, and has very low outgassing. The crucible
    is a cylinder closed at the bottom with a small opening at the top.
  • Ta heater coil: tantalum (or sometimes tungsten) wire wound around the
    crucible delivers the required power without reacting with the PBN.
    The coil is on the OUTSIDE of the crucible; the beam exits from the
    top aperture.
  • Water cooling: absolutely essential — the outer jacket must be water-
    cooled because otherwise radiant heat from the ~1000-1200 °C cell
    interior would damage the CF flange O-rings and desorb contaminants.
  • W-Re thermocouple: W-5%Re / W-26%Re thermocouples are standard for
    high-temp cells because Type K (Chromel/Alumel) fails above ~1300 °C
    in vacuum and can react with the charge. W-Re can be used to 2300 °C.
  • Shutters: pneumatically or magnetically actuated, they MUST physically
    swing aside to open. The sim models a hinged shutter that pivots ~90°
    to clear the aperture — correct mechanism.

Cell temperature → flux relationship:
  Flux F (cm⁻²·s⁻¹) = P(T) / √(2πmkT)
  where P(T) is the equilibrium vapor pressure of the material at the cell
  temperature, m is the atomic mass, k is Boltzmann's constant.
  Vapor pressures:
    Ga at 980 °C:  ~10⁻² Torr  → produces ~1 ML/s Ga flux for a small
                    cell aperture at typical substrate distance
    Al at 1150 °C: ~10⁻² Torr  → similar order
    As at 320 °C:  ~10⁻² Torr  → As4 dominant species, ~15:1 over Ga

The "As4" species is what comes out of an As cell at these temperatures.
Above ~600 °C, As4 partially cracks to As2. Some MBE systems use an As
"cracker" cell that runs the cracking zone at 600-1000 °C to deliberately
convert As4 → As2, which is more reactive for low-temperature growth.

SOURCES
-------
Cho & Arthur (1975), Section 3 — original K-cell design.
Parker (1985), Chapter 2, Section 2.3 — "Effusion Cells."
Herman & Sitter (1996), Chapter 4, Section 4.2 — "Effusion Cells."

Honig, R.E. & Kramer, D.A. (1969). "Vapor pressure data for the solid
  and liquid elements." RCA Review, 30, 285.
    — Source of Ga, Al, As vapor pressure data. The sim's cell temperatures
      are consistent with this data for the target fluxes.

Vendor data:
  SVT Associates (Eden Prairie, MN) K-cell datasheets
  Riber / Veeco effusion cell specifications

DELIBERATE SIMPLIFICATIONS
--------------------------
  • Cell heating is animated as a smooth ramp over each phase. Real K-cells
    have a thermal time constant of ~30-90 s and reach setpoint only after
    minutes. The demo compresses the ~30 min warm-up into ~3 s.
  • Cell temperature → flux is not modeled. The sim assumes the cell
    produces the specified growth rate when its shutter opens. In reality,
    flux depends on cell temp via a strongly nonlinear (Arrhenius) relation,
    and operators calibrate flux vs. temp weekly using the BFM.
  • No thermal crosstalk between cells (adjacent cells heat each other).
  • Cell temperatures shown are target values; the sim does not model PID
    stabilization or overshoot.
  • Crucible charge depletion is not modeled — the sim treats the melt as
    infinite (fine for a demo; a real Ga cell might last months to years).

6. SUBSTRATE MANIPULATOR
================================================================================

WHAT THE SIMULATION SHOWS
-------------------------
  • Bellows section above top flange (8 accordion rings)
  • Rotation motor housing above bellows
  • Vertical rotation shaft descending through chamber to substrate holder
  • Substrate holder (moly block, radius 0.55, height 0.10)
  • Molybdenum heater block behind the holder (glows red when hot)
  • GaAs wafer (0.55 × 0.55) attached to bottom of holder
  • Continuous rotation during growth (14 rpm equivalent)

WHY THIS IS CORRECT
-------------------
Substrate manipulators are the second key MBE component after the cells:
  • Bellows: the manipulator must be adjustable in Z (substrate-to-cell
    distance) without breaking vacuum. Welded stainless steel bellows
    provides linear translation while maintaining the UHV seal.
  • Rotation: uniform growth requires substrate rotation because the beams
    from different cells have slightly different angular distributions.
    Without rotation, a 2-3" wafer would show ±5-10% thickness and ±2-3%
    composition non-uniformity. Rotation at 5-30 rpm is standard.
  • Molybdenum heater: the wafer is radiatively heated from behind by a
    resistively-heated moly (or graphite) block. The wafer is held in
    intimate contact with the heater surface. "Substrate temperature" in
    the sim refers to the temperature of this heater block, which is
    tracked by a thermocouple or (more commonly) an optical pyrometer
    focused on the wafer backside.
  • Wafer attachment: thermally-conductive mounting is essential. Common
    methods: In bonding (In melts at 156 °C, but evaporates slowly above
    500 °C), Ga backside contact, or mechanical clamping. The sim shows
    small clips at the wafer corners representing mechanical clamping.

Wafer rotation rate in the sim: 0.8 rad/s = 7.6 rpm. Real MBE uses 5-30
rpm depending on uniformity requirements. The spec panel says "20 rpm" but
the animation runs slightly slower; treat 20 rpm as the nominal figure.

SOURCES
-------
Parker (1985), Chapter 2, Section 2.5 — "Substrate Manipulators."
Herman & Sitter (1996), Chapter 4, Section 4.4.

DELIBERATE SIMPLIFICATIONS
--------------------------
  • The heater block is shown but its thermal model is lumped into
    "substrate T." Real systems have a two-thermocouple PID (one on heater
    block, one via optical pyrometer on the wafer).
  • Wafer attachment (In bonding, clip mounting) is stylized — the sim
    shows corner clips but doesn't model the thermal resistance this adds.
  • Rotation is continuous and uniform. Real systems sometimes use
    oscillatory (back-and-forth) rotation for better uniformity at the
    wafer edge.
  • Azimuthal adjustment (independent of rotation) is not modeled.
  • Substrate-to-cell distance is fixed in the sim (~4.6 model units ≈
    40 cm at the sim's scale). Real MBE systems allow this to be tuned
    between ~10-20 cm during installation.

7. REFLECTION HIGH-ENERGY ELECTRON DIFFRACTION (RHEED)
================================================================================

WHAT THE SIMULATION SHOWS
-------------------------
  • 15 keV electron gun at right, grazing angle to substrate
  • Fluorescent screen at left, opposite the gun
  • Incident beam and diffracted beam (thin blue tubes) visible when
    the run is in progress
  • Screen glows green, brightness tracks the specular spot intensity
  • Live (00) specular intensity trace in the sidebar — sinusoidal
    oscillation with one cycle per monolayer, damping through the layer

PHYSICS
-------
RHEED is THE diagnostic of MBE. An electron beam at ~10-40 keV strikes
the sample at 0.5-3° (grazing incidence), and the diffracted beams form
a pattern on a phosphor screen. Several features are diagnostic:

  • Streaks vs. spots: streaky pattern = atomically flat surface
    (2D-like); spotty = rough surface (3D islands). Watching the
    transition from streaks to spots reveals roughening.

  • RHEED oscillations: during layer-by-layer growth, the (00)
    specular intensity oscillates with a period of exactly one
    monolayer. The oscillation is strongest when growth proceeds
    by 2D nucleation; it DAMPS as the surface roughens and can be
    restored by brief growth interruption (annealing).

  • Reconstruction: GaAs (100) surfaces show various
    reconstructions (2×4, 4×6, c(4×4), etc.) depending on As coverage
    and temperature. Reading the reconstruction tells you the surface
    stoichiometry in real time.

  • Growth rate: RHEED oscillation frequency × monolayer thickness =
    growth rate in real time. This is one of the primary ways operators
    measure growth rate in situ, without breaking vacuum.

  • Composition: for alloys (AlGaAs), RHEED intensity is different from
    binary (GaAs), and phase differences at interfaces reveal composition
    grading.

IN THE SIMULATION
-----------------
  • The grazing angle is modeled at ~4° (the sim's beam descends slightly
    steeper than a real 1-2° system).
  • The oscillation is a cosine: I(t) = 50 + 42 × cos(2π f t) × decay,
    with decay = 1 - 0.35 × (layer fraction). This models the amplitude
    damping observed in Neave et al. — oscillation amplitude shrinks as
    the growing layer roughens.
  • The oscillation rate is compressed: real 1 ML/s growth gives 1 Hz
    oscillation, which would be a blur in the demo. The sim runs
    ~1 oscillation per second of demo time across a phase that is
    nominally 30-60 min of real time.

Why RHEED is on the screen the whole time (not just during growth):
In a real run, RHEED stays on from the moment the wafer is hot (so
operators can watch the oxide desorption, which shows characteristic
changes in the pattern). The sim shows this by keeping RHEED active
through the entire growth cycle and only fading it during the initial
cold phases.

SOURCES
-------
Cho, A.Y. (1970). "Morphology of epitaxial growth of GaAs by molecular
  beam method." Journal of Applied Physics, 41, 2780.
    — First RHEED observations during MBE growth.

Foxon, C.T. & Joyce, B.A. (1977). "Interaction kinetics of As4 and Ga
  on {100} GaAs surfaces using a modulated molecular beam technique."
  Surface Science, 64, 293-306.
    — Established that RHEED oscillations correspond to monolayer
      filling.

Neave, J.H., Joyce, B.A., Dobson, P.J. & Norton, N. (1983). "Dynamics
  of film growth of GaAs by MBE from RHEED observations." Applied
  Physics A, 31, 1-8.
    — Definitive paper on RHEED oscillation damping and its
      interpretation in terms of surface roughness.

Joyce, B.A., Dobson, P.J., Neave, J.H. et al. (1986). "RHEED studies
  of the growth of GaAs and related compounds." Surface Science, 168,
  110-120.
    — Review of RHEED as a growth diagnostic.

Vendor data:
  k-Space Associates (Dexter, MI) kSA 400 RHEED analysis system
  STAIB Instruments RH-20 RHEED gun

DELIBERATE SIMPLIFICATIONS
--------------------------
  • RHEED oscillation rate is compressed ~400× for demo legibility.
  • Real RHEED patterns (streaks, reconstructions, Kikuchi lines) are
    not shown — only the (00) specular intensity trace, which is what
    operators watch during growth.
  • The damping per layer is not quantitatively modeled (real damping
    depends on growth temperature, V/III ratio, surface orientation).
  • Reconstructions (2×4 → c(4×4) transitions during oxide desorption)
    are not depicted. In a real run, watching the RHEED pattern transition
    during the DEGAS phase is a key check that the oxide is fully gone.
  • Grazing angle is 4° in the sim (slightly steeper than the 1-2° of a
    real system), because at 1° the beam would be too close to the wafer
    surface to be visible at demo scale.
  • The electron energy (15 keV) is representative; real systems use
    10-40 keV depending on intended analysis.

8. BEAM FLUX MONITOR (BFM)
================================================================================

WHAT THE SIMULATION SHOWS
-------------------------
  • Retractable ion-collector gauge on a pivot arm
  • At rest: swung to the side (out of the wafer path)
  • During growth: swings to sit just below the substrate, with an
    ion-collector face oriented upward toward the incoming beams
  • Face glows cyan when it is measuring flux

WHY THIS IS CORRECT
-------------------
The BFM (also called an "ion gauge" in some MBE systems, or a
"beam-flux monitor") is a retractable ion gauge that can be swung into
the growth position — directly under the substrate — to measure the flux
from each cell. Since the wafer is not in place during calibration, the
BFM sits where the wafer would. Operators correlate cell temperature with
measured flux, then withdraw the BFM and load the wafer.

The ion collector current is proportional to the number of ions
produced from the flux, which is proportional to the arrival rate of
neutral atoms. By opening one shutter at a time and reading the current,
you get a per-cell flux measurement. These numbers calibrate the growth
rate (typically 1-5% uncertainty per BFM calibration).

The BFM is retracted during growth because it would otherwise shadow
part of the wafer. Some MBE systems also use a "cross-beam" configuration
where the BFM is permanently mounted at a fixed off-axis position and
calibrated against a movable standard.

SOURCES
-------
Parker (1985), Chapter 2, Section 2.6 — "Flux Monitors."
Herman & Sitter (1996), Chapter 4, Section 4.5.

DELIBERATE SIMPLIFICATIONS
--------------------------
  • The BFM's actual ion current readout is not displayed. Only the
    pivot motion (in = measuring, out = not measuring) is shown.
  • Real BFM calibration runs take 5-30 min per cell and require
    opening one shutter at a time against the closed shutter of the
    others. This is not depicted; the sim jumps from "growth" to "growth
    in progress" without showing the calibration step.
  • BFM position is at the substrate plane in the sim; real BFMs may sit
    slightly below the substrate plane to avoid collisions during
    rotation.

9. LOAD LOCK AND WAFER TRANSFER
================================================================================

WHAT THE SIMULATION SHOWS
-------------------------
  • Small load lock chamber (radius 0.38 × 0.65 long) on the rear side
    of the growth chamber
  • Gate valve at chamber interface
  • End flange with door at the opposite end
  • Load-lock pressure is tracked independently of growth chamber
    pressure
  • Transfer phase: gate valve opens, wafer is carried from lock to
    manipulator, gate valve closes

WHY THIS IS CORRECT
-------------------
The load lock is essential for MBE productivity. Without it, loading each
wafer would require venting the entire chamber to atmosphere, waiting for
pump-down to base pressure (~6-24 hours), and re-baking — a full day per
sample. With a load lock, wafers transfer in minutes and the main chamber
stays under UHV for weeks or months.

Standard workflow:
  1. Load wafer into load lock, close and seal.
  2. Pump load lock to <10⁻⁶ Torr (2-10 min with small turbo pump).
  3. Open gate valve, transfer wafer via magnetic arm or wobble stick.
  4. Close gate valve.
  5. Wafer now on the manipulator, ready for degas.

SOURCES
-------
Parker (1985), Chapter 2, Section 2.7 — "Wafer Transfer."
Herman & Sitter (1996), Chapter 4, Section 4.6.

DELIBERATE SIMPLIFICATIONS
--------------------------
  • The magnetic transfer arm mechanism is not shown; the sim just shows
    the wafer appearing on the manipulator after the load-lock phase.
  • Real transfer takes 30-90 s; the sim compresses to ~2 s.
  • Wafer heating in the load lock (some systems have a small heater here
    for pre-degas) is not modeled.

10. PROCESS CYCLE — THERMAL AND SHUTTER SEQUENCE
================================================================================

WHAT THE SIMULATION SHOWS
-------------------------
15-phase cycle, real time ≈ 4 h 30 min (compress to ~46 s demo):

  1. LOAD LOCK · vent N2               10 min
  2. LOCK PUMPING · turbo              20 min
  3. TRANSFER · to growth chamber       2 min
  4. DEGAS · 200 °C                    30 min
  5. RAMP · 200 → 580 °C               10 min
  6. As OVERPRESSURE                    5 min
  7. GaAs BUFFER · 1.0 µm              60 min
  8. TRANSITION · Al opens              1 min
  9. AlGaAs LOWER CLAD · 0.5 µm        30 min
 10. TRANSITION · Al closes             1 min
 11. GaAs ACTIVE · 0.1 µm               6 min
 12. TRANSITION · Al opens              1 min
 13. AlGaAs UPPER CLAD · 0.5 µm        30 min
 14. ALL SHUTTERS CLOSE                 1 min
 15. COOL DOWN · 580 → 100 °C          60 min

WHY THIS IS CORRECT
-------------------
The thermal sequence follows standard MBE practice:
  • Degas at 200 °C: removes adsorbed water and hydrocarbons from the
    wafer backside before reaching growth temperature. Critical — if
    you skip this, the wafer outgasses into the growth chamber and
    contaminates the epilayer.
  • Ramp to 580 °C: GaAs growth temperature. Too low (<500 °C) = poor
    crystal quality and residual defects from insufficient surface
    diffusion; too high (>620 °C) = Ga re-evaporation, rough surfaces,
    and In desorption (for InGaAs).
  • Cool-down under UHV: prevents thermal stress and crack formation.
    Rapid cooling from 580 °C can shatter a wafer. Standard ramp is
    5-10 °C/min.

The shutter sequence follows standard practice:
  • As first: establishes overpressure before Ga arrives, preventing
    Ga droplets on the bare wafer surface.
  • Ga + As: GaAs buffer growth.
  • Al opens while Ga and As remain: AlGaAs layer begins. Composition
    controlled by Al:Ga flux ratio at the surface (not by shutter timing).
  • Al closes: return to GaAs for the active layer.
  • Al opens again: upper cladding.
  • All shutters close: growth terminates.

The 1-minute "transition" phases are a sim simplification. Real MBE has
no such pause — shutters open and close in <1 s while growth continues.
The sim inserts brief pauses so the narration can keep up with the
layer transitions.

SOURCES

DELIBERATE SIMPLIFICATIONS
--------------------------
  • Phase durations are idealized; real growth has continuous temperature
    ramp and shutter timing rather than discrete phases.
  • The 1-minute "transition" pauses are inserted for demo clarity. Real
    transitions are <1 s.
  • Cool-down time depends strongly on chamber thermal mass and cooling
    method (radiative vs. forced N2). 60 min is on the fast end.
  • The sim does not model the "growth interruption" step (30-120 s with
    As overpressure but no Ga) that operators sometimes use to smooth
    interfaces before critical layers. This is standard practice but
    would clutter the demo.
  • The DEGAS step in the sim reaches 200 °C in 3 s demo. Real systems
    take 15-30 min to reach 200 °C and hold for 15-30 min.

11. EPITAXIAL STACK — DH LASER STRUCTURE
================================================================================

WHAT THE SIMULATION DEPICTS
---------------------------
  Layer                     Composition            Thickness
  ------------------------- ---------------------- ---------
  Substrate                 GaAs (100)             350 µm
  Buffer                    GaAs                    1.0 µm
  Lower cladding            Al0.3Ga0.7As            0.5 µm
  Active layer (DH)         GaAs                    0.1 µm
  Upper cladding            Al0.3Ga0.7As            0.5 µm
  ------------------------------------------------------------
  Total epilayer                                    2.1 µm

WHY THIS IS CORRECT
-------------------
This is the canonical DH laser structure used in both MBE and LPE
fabrication (see the LPE simulation documentation for detail). The
1.0 µm buffer, 0.5 µm claddings, and 0.1 µm active layer are standard
for GaAs/AlGaAs lasers emitting at 850 nm.

Al0.3Ga0.7As is the standard composition because:
  • Δn ≈ 0.2 (index step sufficient for optical confinement)
  • ΔEg ≈ 0.4 eV (electron confinement at 850 nm emission)
  • Lattice matches GaAs (0.15% mismatch — strained but pseudomorphic)

Real DH lasers usually add:
  • Cap layer (GaAs, 50-200 nm) for ohmic contact — not in the sim
    (keeps the stack to the essential 4 layers)
  • Graded layers at the cladding/active interfaces to reduce series
    resistance — not in the sim
  • Multiple quantum wells instead of a single DH active region in
    modern designs — the sim uses the classic single-DH structure

SOURCES
-------
Casey & Panish (1978), Chapter 6.
Kressel & Butler (1977), Chapter 6.
Thompson, G.H.B. (1980). Physics of Semiconductor Laser Devices. Wiley.

DELIBERATE SIMPLIFICATIONS
--------------------------
  • The visual thickness of each layer is exaggerated ~50-100× so the
    layer thicknesses are visible at demo scale. Sidebar shows true µm.
  • Interface abruptness is idealized. Real MBE can achieve 1-2 ML
    interface widths for AlGaAs/GaAs at 580 °C, but they are not
    perfectly abrupt — thermal interdiffusion and surface segregation
    of Al both contribute to a finite interface width. The sim shows
    perfectly sharp interfaces.
  • The buffer layer is nominally a "superlattice" in some designs
    (alternating GaAs/AlGaAs for dislocation filtering) rather than
    bulk GaAs. The sim shows bulk GaAs.
  • No dopants (Si for n-type, Be/C for p-type) are shown. Real laser
    structures have p-i-n doping profiles across the claddings.

13. KNOWN SIMPLIFICATIONS AND VISUAL EXAGGERATIONS
================================================================================

1. TIME COMPRESSION
   The full cycle is ~4 h 30 min real, compressed to ~46 s demo.
   Compression is ~350×. Sidebar shows both real and demo time.

2. RHEED OSCILLATION RATE
   Real growth at 1 ML/s produces 1 oscillation per second, which
   would be a blur on screen. The sim compresses to ~1 oscillation per
   second of demo time. This is the largest single compression
   remaining in the sim (~400×) and is documented in code comments.

3. "MLs grown" COUNTER
   The MLs grown counter advances once per RHEED oscillation ×
   (phase ML count / oscillation count). It is illustrative rather
   than physical — it does not reflect the true 3500+ MLs grown during
   a real 1.0 µm buffer. Treat as a rough visual cue, not a
   calibration quantity.

4. CELL THERMAL LAG
   Cells appear at target temperature with a smooth linear ramp.
   Real K-cells have exponential thermal response with ~60 s time
   constant. Sim compresses ~30 min warm-up into ~3 s.

5. NO REAL PID
   Substrate temperature and cell temperatures follow a linear ramp.
   Real systems use dual-loop PID controllers (with optical pyrometer
   on the substrate) that react to overshoot and stabilize to ±1 °C.

6. INTERFACE ABRUPTNESS
   Sim shows perfectly sharp AlGaAs/GaAs interfaces. Real MBE at
   580 °C achieves 2-4 ML interface widths due to Al segregation and
   thermal interdiffusion. Could be reduced by growth interruption or
   substrate temperature modulation.

7. SURFACE RECONSTRUCTIONS
   RHEED reconstructions (2×4, c(4×4), etc.) are not modeled. In a
   real run, watching the reconstruction change during degas is a
   key diagnostic for oxide desorption.

8. BEAM FLUX CALIBRATION
   BFM swings into place but does not show measurement data or
   calibration. Real calibration is a separate procedure before each
   growth run.

9. WAFER ATTACHMENT
   Wafer sits freely on the manipulator. Real wafers are thermally
   bonded (In, Ga backside) or mechanically clamped. Not modeled.

10. CHAMBER SCALE
    Chamber radius 1.5 units ≈ 27 cm diameter (at ~9 cm/unit if wafer
    is 2"). Real 3" MBE chambers are 30-60 cm diameter. Sim is on the
    small side, correct in proportion but not exact dimension.

11. NO GAS-PHASE SCATTERING
    Atoms travel ballistically from cell to substrate. This is correct
    for MBE (mean free path > chamber dimensions) — the sim is faithful
    here, not simplified. Mentioned for completeness because it's often
    misunderstood as a simplification.

12. NO DEFECT MODELING
    Point defects, dislocations, antiphase boundaries, and other crystal
    defects are not modeled. Real MBE produces high-quality crystals but
    with measurable defect densities (~10^3-10^5 cm⁻² threading
    dislocations) that affect device performance.

13. NO DOPING
    Si, Be, or C dopants not shown. Real laser structures need p-i-n
    junctions with specific doping profiles.

14. REDEPOSITION NOT MODELED
    No material sputter from chamber walls; no backscatter from wafer.
    Minor effect in real MBE (~1% of beam), not modeled.

15. ION GAUGE BAYARD-ALPERT
    Shown as small glowing cylinder. Real BA gauge is more complex
    (grid + filament + collector geometry). Detail is stylized.

15. WHAT YOU CAN TUNE
================================================================================

The simulation's physics is largely hard-coded but the following
parameters can be modified without rewriting the model:

  In the CYCLE array:
    dur:     demo duration of each phase (seconds)
    real:    real-world duration (minutes, shown in narration)
    subT:    substrate temperature during the phase
    gaT, alT, asT:   target cell temperatures
    pEnd:    target chamber pressure
    pLock:   target load-lock pressure
    growth:  { layer: N, rate: R } to start growing layer N at R ML/s

  In the STATE S:
    press:   base chamber pressure (currently 5e-11 Torr)
    pLock:   initial load-lock pressure (currently 760 Torr)

  In the geometry:
    CH_R:    chamber radius
    SUB_Y:   substrate height (controls camera framing)
    LAYER_VIS_SCALE: exaggeration factor for growth layers (currently 0.12)

For teaching different growth modes (InGaAs at lower T, AlGaAs at different
compositions, etc.), the CYCLE array is the right place to change things.
For modeling a different material system entirely, both the CYCLE and the
LAYER_INFO array need new values, and the substrate visual color should be
adjusted (currently tuned for GaAs).

16. SUMMARY
================================================================================

The simulation faithfully models:

  ✓ MBE growth mechanism (ballistic beams, surface diffusion, kinetic growth)
  ✓ UHV environment (5×10⁻¹¹ Torr base, ~2×10⁻¹⁰ working)
  ✓ Water-cooled effusion cells with PBN crucibles and Ta heaters
  ✓ Realistic cell temperatures (Ga 980 °C, Al 1150 °C, As 320 °C)
  ✓ As overpressure requirement (~15:1 As:Ga BEP)
  ✓ Substrate rotation for uniformity (14-20 rpm range)
  ✓ RHEED as layer-by-layer growth diagnostic (oscillation + damping)
  ✓ Beam flux monitor operation (retractable, at substrate plane)
  ✓ Load lock with independent pressure pumping
  ✓ DH laser layer structure (1.0 / 0.5 / 0.1 / 0.5 µm GaAs/AlGaAs)
  ✓ 580 °C growth temperature, 1.0 ML/s growth rate
  ✓ Ion pump + turbo + cryopanel pumping system
  ✓ RGA for residual gas analysis
  ✓ Bake-out depicted (implicitly, via base pressure achieved)
  ✓ Dry N2 venting for load lock

The simulation simplifies:

  ◇ RHEED oscillation rate compressed ~400× for demo legibility
  ◇ "MLs grown" counter is illustrative (counts oscillations, not true MLs)
  ◇ Cell thermal ramp is linear, not exponential; warm-up compressed
  ◇ No real PID feedback (temperature follows scripted ramp)
  ◇ Interface abruptness idealized (real interfaces are 2-4 ML wide)
  ◇ Growth transitions paused for narration (real transitions are <1 s)
  ◇ MLs counter does not track true monolayer equivalents
  ◇ No dopant cells, no cap layer, no graded interfaces
  ◇ RHEED reconstructions not shown (only specular intensity trace)
  ◇ BFM calibration procedure skipped
  ◇ Wafer attachment method simplified (corner clips)
  ◇ Chamber dimensions approximate (small end of typical MBE)
  ◇ No defect model

These simplifications do not affect the growth mechanism or the process
physics. The simulation is faithful to the standard MBE literature and is
suitable for teaching, review, and as a mental model of a real MBE
reactor. It is a companion to the LPE simulation: the same DH laser
structure grown by two fundamentally different techniques, allowing
direct comparison of the growth methods.

The principle differences between MBE and LPE, which the two sims
together illustrate:
  • Environment:  UHV (10⁻¹¹ Torr) vs. atmospheric (1 atm)
  • Growth:       kinetic (atomic beams) vs. thermodynamic (cooling melts)
  • Temperature:  580 °C (low) vs. 780 °C (high)
  • Rate:         1 µm/hr vs. ~0.15 µm/min
  • In-situ diag: RHEED (atomic resolution) vs. none typically
  • Interface:    2-4 ML achievable vs. ~10-100 nm

Both produce high-quality DH laser material. MBE is the modern standard
for production; LPE is largely superseded but historically important and
still used in research.
